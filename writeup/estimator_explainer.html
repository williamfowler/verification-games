Reading FLOPs
off the power rail.

You can't see inside a training job. But the GPU still spends energy for every multiply it does — and that energy leaks out as three signals a watt-meter can read from the outside. Here's how a four-line energy model turns those signals back into a FLOP count, why a neural net makes it sharper, and the catch that shows up when someone tries to hide.

TFLOPs = ( Enet − c·DRAM − d·NVLink − p·t ) / a

measured energy, minus what the bytes and idle overhead cost, divided by the energy of one TFLOP

01 — The measurement problem

Three taps on the outside of the box

The estimator never reads the model, the loss, or the code. It watches the hardware through three sensors that any operator already has, and it has one label to learn from during calibration: the honest FLOP count a known workload printed.

02 — The base estimator

An energy ledger, run backwards

Every joule the GPUs draw is spent on one of a few things. Write that as a balance — energy in equals energy out, term by term — and you get a straight line through the calibration runs:

Enet = a·FLOPs + c·DRAM + d·NVLink + p·t

fit a, c, d, p on benign runs · then solve for FLOPs on a new one

a · FLOPs

Compute energy

The prize. Each TFLOP costs a near-constant a ≈ 3.5 J on this hardware — the marginal energy of arithmetic.

c · DRAM

Memory energy

Joules spent moving bytes to and from HBM, subtracted off using the measured terabytes.

d · NVLink

Interconnect

Energy of the gradient all-reduce between the two GPUs, from the NVLink byte counters.

p · t

Idle overhead

Fixed power the card draws just being resident, times how long the job ran.

Subtract the three non-compute terms from the measured energy, divide by a, and what's left is the FLOP count. That's the whole model — the same four lines the live daemon and the offline evaluator both run.

A surprise worth stating plainly: the byte terms don't improve accuracy on honest runs. Power alone (a 2-parameter model) generalizes slightly better than the 3- or 4-parameter versions that add DRAM and NVLink. Those extra signals earn their place elsewhere — as consistency tripwires against tampering — not in the point estimate.

03 — Calibrated accuracy

About a sixth off, on a workload it never saw

The honest test is held-out: calibrate on some workloads, predict a different one, repeat over many splits. Because every workload is run ten times, each split draws a fresh trace — so the number reflects generalization, not memorization.

Median held-out error, power-only: about 13%. Good enough to bracket a training run's scale — but the error isn't random. It bends with the workload, and that bend is the opening a neural net can close.

04 — The neural upgrade

The cost of a FLOP isn't a constant

The base model bets that every TFLOP costs the same a joules. It doesn't. A big, dense matrix multiply runs the tensor cores hotter and cheaper per FLOP than a small awkward one — so the true joules-per-FLOP drifts with the shape of the work. A single constant can't track that drift. A small network can.

The design keeps the physics as a backbone and lets the net learn only the correction — a multiplier centered on 1, driven by dimensionless intensity features (power level, energy-per-byte, interconnect-per-byte) that say nothing about size, only about efficiency:

TFLOPs = est2p × exp(&thinspace;g(z)&thinspace;)

physics estimate × a learned efficiency correction ·  g(z)=0 falls back to the base model

05 — Red team

The sharper tool is the easier one to fool

Accuracy on honest runs is only half the job. This estimator also has to hold up when a tenant wants to under-report — to run real FLOPs that read low. So we replayed the adversarial workloads through the neural estimator and watched the signed error.

The base model's rigidity turns out to be a defense. It assumes one fixed cost per FLOP, so the most an attacker can bend it is by making real FLOPs genuinely cheaper. The net learned that efficiency varies — and an attacker who pushes the workload into an efficient corner it never saw in training gets that flexibility working for them. Inflate the batch far past the calibration range and the net extrapolates its own correction straight into the under-report:

One consolation, and it's a design lesson: the physics-anchored net is consistently less foolable than the plain one — the energy backbone caps how far the learned correction can run. Keeping the model close to a mechanism you trust buys robustness back.

The tradeoff

More accurate on the honest. More exposed to the adversary.

The neural estimator is the better meter — ~20% tighter on workloads no one is trying to game. But a FLOP verifier lives in both worlds, and there the flexibility that won accuracy becomes a fresh lever: every place the net learned that a FLOP can be cheap is a place an attacker can make FLOPs read cheap.

So the choice isn't "which is more accurate." It's whether the deployment is a cooperative meter or a contested one. Trusted telemetry: take the net. Adversarial attribution: the rigid model's single constant is the safer floor — or anchor the net to the physics and give up a little of its edge to keep the rest honest.
